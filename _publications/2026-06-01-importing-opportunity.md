<p>Import competition is known to hurt manufacturing workers, but its effects on their children have been largely overlooked. These effects determine whether a trade shock ends with one generation or reaches the next, shaping how much a child’s prospects depend on family background. When the China Shock hit Canadian manufacturing workers, shouldn't it have made family background matter more for their children? Poorer families have little to draw on, while richer ones can insulate theirs. Using matched employer-employee data that link children to their parents, I find the opposite: the shock raises intergenerational income mobility. In the hardest-hit families, where a child ends up depends about 20% less on where their parents stood. Children from poorer families climb the economic ladder; they escape by moving out of their parents’ industries into ones where family background matters less and pay is about as good. Children from richer families fall; they stood to follow their parents into high-paying manufacturing industries, and the shock takes those jobs away.</p>
